The firm — since 1989

I learned the trade in my father's office.

He founded the firm in 1989 in the Italian neighbourhood of East Montreal, and it has worked mostly on residential buildings since. He was my first mentor in architecture: I worked and interned at his office as an apprentice, saw how it runs, and learned the business of construction in Quebec.

What they told me

SketchUp gives renders, views and the massing. Remodelling is long. When the model is out of date, a wrong window is fixed in Photoshop.

The real problem

The drawings change every week. Nobody can say why a year later.

The client, the developer, the engineers and the city call to move a wall or cut a unit. The edit goes into the plan. The reason stays in a phone call.

Ideation — October 24–26

I generated the flows with AI, then chose on a board.

I worked out the general concepts with Claude, wrote the user flows as text, then had ChatGPT draw each step as a screen. I plotted every screen on one Figma board, one section per day, one row per flow.

With the interviews in mind, I marked each flow: green if it would help the office, red if not. The green ones I built in Claude Code as prototypes with real clicks.

Reframes 1 and 2

I moved the product away from design and toward the work nobody wants to redo.

REFRAME 1 — OCT 24

Not a design tool

Designing is the part architects enjoy and trust themselves with. The pain is in the repetitive reading of drawings: schedules, counts, checks. That is where software earns trust.

REFRAME 2 — OCT 25

Not a detail library

My first direction was a library of construction details. I dropped it after looking at where the office's knowledge lives: in 2D unit plans. The unit schedule is the document rebuilt most often by hand.

02What the city asksCodes

Finding the article was easy. Applying it to this project was the work.

They said
Finding the article is easy. Understanding what it means for this project is the slow part.Guy — interview, Oct 26
Problem
Seven rules from three bodies of law sat in one list, split by a grey caps line that was lost on scroll.
Realization
Across 48 presentations, the card had to show the measurement against the requirement, and what is missing, before anyone reads the quote. Each source needed its own place.
Decision
One card per article: a small drawing of the rule, the quote, the edition, and « manque pour conclure ». Each body of rules has its own tab; the official text opens at the article.

03Finding your wayWords

In Québec, a « croisement » is a street corner.

They said
The term « Distribution » is not intuitive. Avoid file words like « .csv ».Guy — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
The interface called a place where two drawings disagree a « croisement ».
Realization
Read in the office's language, a « croisement » is a street intersection. Words are interface: each candidate had to be read in place, not in a list.
Decision
« Conflit », chosen from six candidates tried in the header badge, the marker card and the page title. One warning triangle and one red, everywhere.
04Seeing the drawingRoom for the drawing

The drawing being read filled a fifth of the screen.

They said
Allow users to resize the viewing panels. Increase the maximum zoom.Benoît — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
A list, two drawings and an inspector shared one white card. The plan being read got about a fifth of it.
Realization
Ten live gestures, each with the drawing's share measured in place: more room only helps if the way back stays in sight.
Decision
Zoom became a small « − 100 % + » pill in the free corner, the whole sheet sits in a corner map, and any pane folds into a strip that keeps its name.
05Seeing the drawingDrawing layers

Seven drawings per floor. Most of the time you need one.

They said
Opacity controls are unclear. Start every drawing at 100 %.Guy — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
Seven drawings per floor, in a 272 px rail that took almost a full column from the plan.
Realization
Several drawings matter together only to see where they contradict, which Benoît did by overlaying them in his head. The rest of the time, you read one.
Decision
A strip of thumbnails beside the plan. A green badge lays a drawing on the sheet, and once two are on, the plan marks their conflicts in red.
06Seeing the drawing3D model

Labels on a turning building jumped by up to 577 pixels.

They said
Let the client turn the building. Useful in meetings, not during production.Guy — interview, Oct 26
Problem
Level labels on the 3D model jumped while it orbited: up to 577 px in a slow scripted orbit.
Realization
Six placements, then six treatments of the active level alone, all on one shared camera: the level tabs already name the floor. The model's job is to select and to show façades, not to label.
Decision

07The work around the drawingQuantities

Quantities lived on another screen, and every unit was listed twice.

They said
Show the 3½ / 4½ / 5½ mix and the total net area. Quantities per façade beat totals.Benoît — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
Quantities sat on a separate overview screen. On a floor, the pane listed the fifteen units twice: once for areas, once for openings.
Realization
The list of units already is the table of quantities. Six ways to write each unit once showed a switch was enough.
Decision
The unit list is the area table: net ft², m², share, then net, circulation and gross, with the mix bar above and a Superficies / Ouvertures switch. Materials get a table per façade.
08The work around the drawingSchedule

A week of work crosses four projects. The schedule cannot live inside one.

They said
Clicking a calendar date should show the project information. Use red and orange for priority, not green.Guy — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
Échéancier sat among one project's tabs, and its month grid only answered « when is it due ».
Realization
Six calendars judged on four questions: what is due, what is late, what is mine, how long until filing. And a week of work crosses four projects, so the schedule cannot live inside one.
Decision
Échéancier sits top right on every screen: a calendar and one to-do list grouped by day. Each task belongs to one of four people and points at its article. Red only for what is late.
09Sharing and using itComments

What a comment is attached to decides whether it is still true tomorrow.

They said
The commenting system is unclear.Guy — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
A shared link lets guests comment: contractor, surveyor, borough. The studio also writes notes to itself. The two must never mix.
Realization
What a comment hangs on decides whether it is still true after the next revision. And an internal note must not be greyed in the shared link; it must not be there at all.
Decision
Comments pin to the drawing and carry their unit and sheet. « À l'interne » or « Partagée » is chosen while writing; internal notes are not rendered in the shared link.
10Sharing and using itPanes

Make a pane narrow, and its table was cut at the edge.

They said
Allow users to resize the viewing panels.Benoît — test of prototype 2, Oct 28
Problem
Once panes resized, dragging the units pane narrower cropped the share column, then half of m², then the figures.
Realization
Resizing must never crop a number. And once a pane was folded, nothing said it could be reopened.
Decision
A pane stops at the width its table needs, with a « butée » tag. Narrower, it folds into a strip that keeps its icon, its title and the button that reopens it.

Trade-offs

What I chose, what I gave up for it, and why the cost was worth paying.

Trade-off 1 — Tools

Keep AutoCAD and SketchUp. No Revit.

Cost. This prototype alone loads 32 PDF sheets: 98 MB, up to 8 MB each. Storing, indexing and drawing an archive of them is the hardest part of the backend.

Trade-off 2 — Integration

Simulate AutoCAD and SketchUp. Make every handoff real.

Cost. No live connection yet, and every simulated screen says so. Plug-ins are the next engineering task.

Trade-off 3 — Trust

Frank proposes changes. A person applies them in the drawing.

Cost. One extra step per change. One broken plan would have cost the office's trust.

Trade-off 4 — The drawing comes first

The plan is shown as drawn. Frank only adds a layer on top.

Cost. No attractive AI-generated views. Nothing on screen the office would have to un-believe.

Trade-off 5 — Codes

An early warning, never a verdict.

Instead of pass or fail, the feature that demos best. Cost. A weaker demo, but an answer an architect can sign.

Trade-off 6 — Subtraction

Much of prototype 3 is what I took out.

Reflection

What I would keep doing.

Users ask for chores, not features

The best ideas were the dullest: counts, tables, the right word. Taking them seriously made the product credible to the people using it.

Build in the real material

Testing on the office's own drawings found problems no mockup could: zoom direction, sheet registration, a 0.3 window.

Say what the product cannot do

Simulated screens labelled simulated. Codes that name what is missing. Trust was the feature.
